From Switches to the Intel 8088

Zero prior hardware knowledge required: bits, bytes, why assembly exists, and how a CPU talks to memory.

Your Win For This Lesson

1. Start at Rock Bottom: Bits & Bytes

Inside a computer, there are no English letters, no Python, and no decimal digits like 5 or 9. At the lowest level, a computer only understands electricity ON or OFF.

We call one tiny ON/OFF switch a bit: 0 means OFF, 1 means ON. One switch can only say two things (0 or 1), which isn't very useful alone. So computers group 8 bits together and call that bundle a byte.

With 8 switches in a row, you can make 256 different patterns—representing any number from 0 to 255, or a single letter like A. Try flipping the 8 switches below right now:

2. Why Do We Learn Assembly Language?

Because a CPU only understands raw 0s and 1s (called machine code), giving it an instruction like "add two numbers" originally looked like 00000011 11000011. Humans make mistakes reading walls of 0s and 1s.

Assembly Language simply gives those 0s and 1s short human names—like MOV (move data) and ADD (add numbers). High-level languages like C++ or Python hide the hardware from you; Assembly removes the curtain so you talk directly to the processor (VU CS401 Lecture 1).

3. The Kitchen Analogy: CPU, Registers & Memory

Imagine a kitchen with two spaces:

  • Memory (RAM) is the giant pantry. It has thousands of numbered lockers (Locker #0, Locker #1, Locker #2…), and each locker holds 1 byte. The pantry is "dumb"—it just stores things. It cannot chop or cook.
  • The CPU is the chef. The chef does all the work (adding, comparing, deciding).
  • Registers are the chef's tiny cutting board. Walking back and forth to the pantry for every tiny step is slow. So the CPU keeps a handful of tiny, super-fast storage slots right inside itself to hold the numbers it is actively working on.

4. The Three Buses: How the Chef Talks to the Pantry

How does the CPU actually fetch a byte from Locker #5 in RAM, or store a byte back into Locker #12? Through bundles of copper wires called system buses (Intel 8086 Family User's Manual). Think of a bus as a multi-lane highway where each lane is 1 wire carrying 1 bit (0 or 1):

Bus Plain-English Job Direction
Address Bus Says WHERE: sends the locker number (e.g., "Open Locker #5!"). One-way (CPU → RAM)
Data Bus Carries WHAT: the actual byte moving in or out of that locker. Two-way (CPU ↔ RAM)
Control Bus Says WHICH ACTION: tells RAM whether to READ (give me the byte) or WRITE (save this byte). Control & timing signals

5. Meet Your CPU: The Intel 8088 (iAPX88)

In CS401, you learn Assembly on the Intel 8088 (also called iAPX88)—the exact chip used in the very first IBM Personal Computer. Why do universities still teach it? Because it is simple enough to hold completely in your head, yet every modern Intel/AMD laptop today is a direct descendant of it!

Here are the four numbers about the 8088 that show up constantly in VU CS401 quizzes:

16-bit Inside the CPU
14 Internal Registers
20-bit Address Bus (1 MB)
8-bit External Data Bus

What do those wire counts mean in plain English?

  • 8-bit Data Bus: There are 8 data wires, so the 8088 moves 1 byte (8 bits) at a time to or from RAM.
  • 20-bit Address Bus: There are 20 address wires. Since each wire can be 0 or 1, 20 wires can create 220 = 1,048,576 unique locker numbers—which is 1 Megabyte (1 MB) of RAM!
  • 16-bit Internal / 14 Registers: Inside the chip, its 14 cutting-board slots (registers) are 16 bits wide (2 bytes each).
